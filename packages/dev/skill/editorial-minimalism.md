# Editorial Minimalism

The page is a page. A person reads it, top to bottom, and the only thing between
them and the words is the design.

This is the direction for a product whose content is the product: a publication,
a documentation set, a research tool, a long report. It is the least suitable
direction for a dense dashboard, and it is not a failure of the style that a
dashboard looks wrong in it. The style wants a measure, a leading, and a reader.

## The type voice

A serif sets the headings and often the body as well. Where the interface needs
a sans, a quiet humanist sans does the labels and the controls, and the serif
keeps the prose.

The scale is the style's instrument. The steps are far enough apart that each
one is a decision, and the top of the scale is a real jump: a display step
measured in multiples of the body, not in percentages. The body is set at a
comfortable size with generous leading, and the measure is held between sixty
and seventy-two characters.

Labels are set small and tracked open, in caps or in a small sans, the way a
printed page marks a section. Figures are tabular wherever a column of numbers
is read.

## The palette direction

Paper, not white. A warm off-white around `#faf8f4`, a paper that has a
temperature, and ink that is warm too: a near-black with a little brown or olive
in it, never `#000`.

One ink accent, and it is used the way a printed page uses a second colour: the
links, and one mark per page. A second, quieter colour may appear on the odd
rule or the odd tag, and it stays close to the accent in weight, so the page
still reads as one ink plus a paper.

The neutrals do the work. There is no grey ramp with a colour hidden in it; the
ramp is the paper, from the page down to the faintest rule.

## The shape

Nothing. Square or barely rounded, at most a couple of pixels, because paper has
no corners and a printed page does not round its own rules. The only exceptions
are the few controls that are genuinely pills, and an exception that appears
three times is a rule.

## The depth

None at all. Not a soft shadow, not a hard one. Separation comes from rules,
from whitespace, and from the ink's weight: a rule at one pixel, a column of
air, a heading set larger than the body.

This is the style where a shadow is a mistake, because a shadow says an object
is floating above a surface, and a page is not floating above anything.

## The motion

Almost none. A short fade, a hundred and twenty to two hundred milliseconds, on
a link's underline or on a small reveal. Nothing moves that was not asked to
move, and nothing that moves travels any distance.

The rule of thumb: if the reader would notice the motion while reading, it is
too much.

## The density

Reading density. A narrow measure, a leading around one and a half to one and
three quarters, and generous space above and below every block of text. A page
of this style is shorter than a page of a dashboard, and that is correct.

## The decisions that make it recognisable

1. **A warm paper ground and warm ink.** Never pure white on pure black. The
   tint is the whole difference between editorial and a print-default
   stylesheet.
2. **A serif that sets the body, and a display step at the top of the scale**
   that is several times the body rather than a little more.
3. **Rules and space instead of boxes and shadows.** Where a dashboard would put
   a card with a border and a shadow, this style puts a rule and some air.

## How it drifts into the house style

It drifts when the paper turns to a pure white and the ink to a pure black,
because the temperature was the style. It drifts when a card with a shadow
appears, because that is the system taking over from the page. And it drifts
when the type goes all sans: editorial minimalism without a serif is a landing
page, and a landing page is not wrong, it is just not this.

The tell is the shadow. One shadow, and the paper is a surface.
